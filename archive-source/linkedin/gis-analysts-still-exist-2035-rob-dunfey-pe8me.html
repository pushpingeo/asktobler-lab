Will "GIS Analysts" still exist in 2035?

Created on 2025-07-27 15:09

Published on 2025-07-31 15:43

The people? Hopefully. The role? I’m not so sure.

If you haven’t felt the surge in productivity from tools like Copilot or ChatGPT recently, I’d bet you haven’t given them a proper try. The pace of change is staggering — and accelerating.

There’s a quote from Bill Gates I often return to:

“We always overestimate the change that will occur in the next two years and underestimate the change that will occur in the next ten.”

We’re in one of those ten-year stretches right now — and if you work in tech or data, you can feel it.

AI Is Eating Entry-Level Roles

It’s no surprise we’re seeing headlines about AI disrupting entry-level positions. That gave me pause — how would I have fared if I were starting out today?

Rob Dunfey vs Grok… who wins?

Let’s rewind to the start of my own journey. The year was 2001. The 21st century was barely a year old when I showed up — suited and booted — at my local oil refinery, Stanlow, Ellesmere Port, for my very first job. September 17th, to be exact.

It was an inflection point for ties — risky to go without one, but only one other colleague would be wearing one. In my case, the security guard radioed in my arrival with a chuckle:

“Your lighthouse in the desert has arrived.”

Chad Earle collected me from reception and let me in on the joke, “Graduates, stand out a mile — and are absolutely useless.” I took off the tie.

Learning to Learn

I had a degree in Environmental Science — back when it wasn’t trendy, or tied to virtuous-sounding roles in Climate, Nature and Sustainability. You got paid for fixing real environmental problems.

My job? £20,000 for 12 months work to support environmental compliance and analysis using something called “GIS.” My first month was spent teaching myself ArcView and Visual Basic (from a book). After that, my task was to:

Model groundwater dispersion using raster math in ArcGIS, implementing the Domenico equation — each term represented as a raster layer.

This took me deep into a world of object model diagrams, over 3,000 objects, and forums where generous strangers — like Kirk Kuykendall — helped newbies like me understand why points, lines, and polygons can’t live in the same layer (forever grateful Kirk!).

That year, I learned far more than software. I learned how to learn. I developed a skill that got me paid. And I became “a GIS Analyst.”

Fast-Forward to 2025

Out of curiosity — and maybe a touch of nostalgia — I recently asked Grok, ChatGPT & Gemini to solve that same problem from 2001: model the Domenico equation using raster math in GIS.

The result? Some QC would be required on the equation, to guard against hallucinations, but it's incredible. The code looked good and the explanation behind it was clear, accurate, and elegant.

There’s no doubt in my mind: These tools could easily have replaced the need to hire me back then. But equally, they would’ve made me 10x more efficient.

It’s 50/50 — disruptive and empowering, all at once.

So What Becomes of the Role?

Back then, GIS was niche and highly technical. Now, spatial is everywhere — in our apps, our dashboards, and our AI models. Tools have evolved. Coding has changed. Expectations have shifted.

But in a world of foundation models, vibe coding, no-code platforms, and AI copilots that can write SQL & Python and classify imagery…I find myself wondering:

What will the role of the GIS Analyst look like in 2035?

Will it even exist? Or will it morph into something new — less about spatial analysis and more about business translation, geospatial strategy, or AI prompt engineering? Can you do strategy without having done the foundational work? How long will we need prompt engineering?

What Do You Think?

I don’t have all the answers, but I’m interested in the conversation. For those of you starting your geospatial careers now — how are you navigating this new era? For the veterans — what are you seeing change most rapidly?

And for everyone: What are we doing to ensure that people — not just roles — continue to grow, contribute, and stay relevant?
